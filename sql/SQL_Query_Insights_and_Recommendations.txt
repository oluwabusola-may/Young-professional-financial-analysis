THE STATE OF PERSONAL FINANCE AMONG YOUNG NIGERIAN PROFESSIONALS (2026)
SQL Queries Insights & Recommendations
================================================================================

SECTION 1: DEMOGRAPHICS & SEGMENTATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1a. Distribution by employment type
Insight: Full-time employees make up the largest share of respondents (35.4%),
followed by freelancers (21.1%) and self-employed (17.5%) together, non-
traditional earners (freelance + self-employed + business owner) make up over
half the sample (51.3%).
Recommendation: Financial products should be designed with income variability
in mind, not just standard payroll deduction models as flexible/manual savings
triggers matter as much as auto-debit for salaried staff.

Q1b. Distribution by income frequency
Insight: Just over half of respondents (55.5%) earn on a regular weekly/monthly
salary, while 44.5% have irregular or multiple income streams which shows that a substantial
minority whose cash flow doesn't fit typical banking products.
Recommendation: Banks/fintechs should offer income-smoothing tools (e.g.,
flexible auto-save that adjusts to inflows) rather than fixed-date debit
products, to serve the irregular-income segment.

Q2. Average income by employment type
Insight: Freelancers and self-employed respondents report the highest average
incomes (₦126k and ₦121k), notably higher than full-time employees (₦106k), thereby
challenging the assumption that formal employment guarantees higher earnings.
Recommendation: Fintechs shouldn't underweight freelancers/self-employed as a
target segment for premium products just because they lack a "steady
paycheck" since their income potential is comparable or higher.


SECTION 2: SAVINGS BEHAVIOR
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q3. Savings bracket distribution
Insight: Over half of respondents (53.9%) save just 1–10% of their income, and
only 23.1% save more than 20% whivh shows savings behavior skews low across the sample.
Recommendation: Product design should focus on nudging respondents from the
1–10% bracket upward (e.g., gradual round-up savings, gamified savings
streaks) rather than assuming an all-or-nothing savings habit.

Q4. Income frequency vs. savings rate
Insight: Respondents with irregular income save at higher rates in the 1–10%
bracket (59.3%), but are less likely to reach the 21%+ bracket (19.8%)
compared to those on regular salaries (24.6%) — steady income enables higher-
tier saving.
Recommendation: Fintechs targeting gig/freelance earners should prioritize
consistency-building tools (e.g., income averaging, "pay yourself first"
auto-transfers) since irregular earners save often but rarely reach high
savings tiers.

Q5. Average Wellness Score by savings bracket
Insight: Wellness Score rises with savings rate up to a point as the 11–20%
bracket has the highest average score (58), while both non-savers (0%, avg 47)
and the highest savers (21%+, avg 39) score lower, suggesting the top-saving
group may be sacrificing other wellness dimensions (e.g., no emergency fund,
no investing) to hit high savings rates.
Recommendation: Don't market "save more" as a blanket solution but highlight
balanced financial health (savings + emergency fund + investing together)
rather than savings rate alone as the goal.


SECTION 3: EMERGENCY FUNDS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q6. % with an emergency fund
Insight: 58.1% of respondents have an emergency fund, but that still leaves
41.9% with over 4 in 10 still exposed to a single income shock.
Recommendation: Fintechs should position emergency-fund-building as a starter
product (e.g., auto-save "safety net" tiers) before cross-selling investment
products to the uncovered 42%.

Q7. Months of expenses covered (among those with a fund)
Insight: Most funded respondents (67.6%) can cover 3 months or less of
expenses, and only 10.6% have 6+ months since even those who've "started" an
emergency fund are thin, not fully buffered.
Recommendation: Products should nudge existing emergency-fund holders toward
the 3–6 month benchmark, not just toward opening a fund because coverage depth
matters as much as having one.

Q8. Emergency fund by employment type
Insight: Full-time employees have the highest emergency fund coverage
(67.0%), while unemployed/job-seeking respondents have the lowest by far
(34.1%) which shows a ~33-point gap tied directly to income stability.
Recommendation: Target the unemployed/job-seeking and freelancer segments
with low-barrier, small-amount auto-save products, since traditional "save a
fixed % monthly" advice doesn't fit their income pattern.


SECTION 4: INVESTING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q9. % currently investing
Insight: Only 42.2% of respondents are currently investing, meaning the
majority (57.8%) have no investment activity at all.
Recommendation: There's a clear market gap for beginner-friendly, low-minimum
investment products (e.g., micro-investing apps) targeted at the non-
investing majority, especially paired with financial literacy nudges.

Q10. Most common investment types (among investors)
Insight: Stocks lead as the top investment type (20.0% of investors), but the
remaining money is spread thin across business, real estate, mutual funds,
and crypto (6–10% each) which shows that no single "default" investment vehicle dominates.
Recommendation: Investment products targeting this segment should lead with
stocks/equity access (since that's already familiar) while bundling in easy
on-ramps to diversify (real estate fractional ownership, mutual fund
baskets) rather than assuming crypto is the entry point.

Q11. Most common reason for not investing
Insight: Nearly half of non-investors (47.2%) cite not earning enough as the
barrier, and a further 27.5% simply "haven't gotten to it together, ~75%
aren't investing due to income/inertia rather than distrust or lack of
understanding (only 14.6% cite not understanding it).
Recommendation: Micro-investing products with very low minimums (e.g.,
round-ups, ₦500 entry points) will do more to convert this segment than
investor education campaigns, hence the barrier is access and activation energy,
not knowledge.

Q12. Investing status by age range and employment type
Insight: Investing participation is fairly flat across age groups (39.5%–
48.9%, no clear age trend), but employment type shows a sharp divide which shows self-
employed (53.7%) and freelancers (46.2%) invest at nearly double the rate of
the unemployed/job-seeking (22.0%).
Recommendation: Segment investment product marketing by employment/income
stability rather than age, since age is not a useful predictor here, but
employment type is.


SECTION 5: BUDGETING & FINANCIAL LITERACY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q13. Budgeting confidence overall and by age range
Insight: Overall budgeting confidence sits low-to-moderate, and confidence
rises slightly with age since 18–24 year-olds report the lowest confidence
(3.56), peaking at 30–34 (3.78) before dipping again at 35+ (3.50).
Recommendation: Target budgeting-confidence tools (guided budgeting
templates, spend-tracking nudges) specifically at the youngest segment
(18–24), who represent both the largest respondent group and the least
confident budgeters.

Q14. Understanding of inflation/compound interest
Insight: 60.7% of respondents correctly understand both inflation and
compound interest concepts, but 12.7% understand neither which shows a meaningful
literacy gap in a sample that's otherwise financially engaged enough to take
this survey.
Recommendation: Embed just-in-time financial literacy content (e.g., a
30-second explainer before opening a savings/investment product) rather than
assuming baseline literacy and especially for the ~39% who don't fully grasp
both concepts.

Q15. Financial literacy score by Wellness Score category
Insight: Financial literacy tracks cleanly with wellness category since Thriving
respondents average 13.3/15, dropping steadily through Stable (11.6), At
Risk (10.7), down to Vulnerable (9.3). The gap between Thriving and
Vulnerable (~4 points) confirms literacy isn't just one input among many, but
it's also a consistent marker of overall financial health.
Recommendation: Financial literacy interventions (explainer content, in-app
nudges on budgeting/inflation concepts) are a legitimate lever for improving
wellness outcomes, not just a "nice to have", therefore fintechs should treat literacy
education as core product infrastructure, not an add-on blog post.


SECTION 6: OVERALL WELLNESS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q16. Distribution across Wellness Score bands
Insight: The majority of respondents fall into "Vulnerable" (115, ~37%) or
"At Risk" (114, ~37%) since nearly 3 in 4 young Nigerian professionals are in the
bottom two wellness bands, while only 12 respondents (~4%) are "Thriving."
Recommendation: This is the headline stat for the report, therefore lead with it. It
makes the strongest case to banks/fintechs that there's a large underserved
market needing foundational financial products, not just premium/investment
offerings.

Q17. Most common "lowest scoring area"
Insight: Emergency Fund is the single most common weak spot, cited as the
lowest-scoring area for 148 respondents, hence it is the largest pain point by far
compared to other components.
Recommendation: If a bank/fintech had to pick one product to prioritize for
this demographic, it should be an emergency-savings tool, because it's the most
common structural gap, not budgeting or literacy.

Q18. Wellness Score by state/region, employment type, income bracket
Insight: Wellness Score varies more by income than by geography or job type, it shows
respondents earning ₦500k–1m average 57.1, more than double those earning
under ₦100k (40.0); by employment type, self-employed lead (51.7) while
unemployed/job-seeking trail badly (36.0); state-level results (e.g., Enugu
at 77.5, n=2) are too thin to be reliable and should be treated as
directional only.
Recommendation: Frame wellness gaps primarily around income tier and
employment stability in the report, theefore flag state-level breakdowns as
sample-size-limited rather than presenting them as solid regional findings.


SECTION 7: GOALS & BEHAVIOR
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Q19. Most common financial goals
Insight: "Business" is the dominant stated goal (122 respondents, well ahead
of Home Ownership at 68 and Debt Freedom at 38). It shows that young Nigerian
professionals are more oriented toward entrepreneurship/income growth than
debt payoff or homeownership.
Recommendation: Fintechs should pair financial wellness products with
business-funding or business-savings features (e.g., dedicated "business
goal" savings pots, small business loan pathways) rather than assuming
mortgage or debt products are the top cross-sell opportunity.
